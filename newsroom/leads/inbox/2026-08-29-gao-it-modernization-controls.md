## Hypothesis

GAO's public performance and budget records may not allow an outside reader to reconcile the progress of its IT-modernization initiatives with the total cost of the program after the agency moved from a five-year plan to an ongoing strategy.

## What would be new

The new finding would compare the OIG's count of 39 completed initiatives out of 61 as of May 2026 and its warning that smaller initiatives were grouped under larger projects with the earlier performance plan's stated modernization roadmap, targets, and budget disclosures, testing whether public records expose a measurable cost-and-progress trail.

## Primary excerpts captured

- OIG-26-2, “Fast Facts” and “What the OIG Found”: GAO says it moved from a five-year modernization plan to an ongoing strategy less than 18 months after the plan was finalized; as of May 2026, 39 of 61 initiatives were reported complete, while some smaller initiatives were tracked under larger projects. URL: https://www.gao.gov/products/oig-26-2
- GAO-23-900466, “Challenge: Establishing IT Systems for the Future of Work”: the FY2024 performance plan says the five-year IT modernization plan was adopted in FY2022, describes a four-year enterprise-content-management roadmap, and reports an 80-percent FY2024 target for the IT-tools measure against 56 percent actual performance in FY2022. URL: https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-23-900466.pdf

## Human stake

Public accountability depends on whether the congressional watchdog can show what its own modernization spending delivered.

## Why it might be wrong

The OIG's control findings may concern documentation and reporting rather than poor delivery; itemized costs or current internal dashboards may exist in public records not yet retrieved, and the two documents cover different planning periods and measures.

## Series potential

Yes. A series could compare the OIG's control findings with later GAO budget, procurement, and performance records, without treating the audit summary as proof of waste or failure.
